2026 U.S. Payroll Tax Rates, Wage Bases & Deadlines — Complete Reference for Employers

By PayrollTaxReform.com · Updated November 11, 2026 · Educational information — not tax advice · Sources: IRS & SSA — details at footnotes

One page with the federal payroll-tax numbers employers actually use: the 2026 Social Security wage base ($184,500), the 2026 FUTA net rate (0.6%), the Medicare thresholds, and the key filing and deposit deadlines — pulled from the primary IRS and SSA publications. If you are used to ranking salaries, our companion study answers a narrower question: what does it cost in employer-side federal tax, in dollars, to employ someone at a given salary? Read it at Employer Payroll Tax by Salary (2026): What Each Salary Costs in Federal Taxes.

A note on timing — a reference page, not a calendar

This page is a reference for the 2026 tax year, not a calendar-year news post. The filing deadlines for the 2026 tax year extend into early 2027, because fourth-quarter Form 941, Form W-2/W-3, Form 940 and Form 944/945 all cover 2026 but are due in early 2027. If you landed here in early 2027 looking for the January 15 deadline, it is in Table 4.

Key findings

A $50,000 employee costs $3,867 a year in employer federal payroll tax — an effective rate of 7.73%.The total is Social Security (6.2% of $50,000 = $3,100) + Medicare (1.45% of $50,000 = $725) + FUTA net (0.6% of $7,000 = $42). The Additional Medicare tax is employee-only and does not change the employer cost.
The effective employer rate peaks at low salaries and falls once the Social Security wage base is passed.At $20,000 the rate is 7.86%; at $184,500 it is 7.67%; at $500,000 it is 3.75%, because only Medicare (1.45%) still applies.
The Social Security wage base rose 4.8% in 2026 to $184,500.Maximum employer Social Security is $11,439.00 per employee — up $520.80 from 2025 ($10,918.20).
Rates did not change in 2026 — the wage base is what moves.Social Security is 6.2% for employer and employee up to the wage base; Medicare is 1.45% each with no wage-base limit; FUTA is 6.0% gross / 0.6% net on the first $7,000.

Federal payroll taxes — the baseline for every employee — 2026

The employer and employee each pay a share of the tax on each worker's wages. The employer share is an additional cost on top of the employee's gross pay — it does not reduce the employee's pay.

Sources: IRS Publication 15; IRS: Social Security and Medicare withholding rates; SSA wage base; Form 940; IRS: Federal unemployment tax; IRS: Additional Medicare tax; IRS Publication 15 (holiday and payroll schedules); IRS Publication 15 (minimum wage base); IRS Publication 15 (FUTA credit) and IRS Publication 15 (FUTA credit reduction).

What each line means — and where the numbers come from

  • Social Security (OASDI) — 6.2% each, up to $184,500. The employer pays 6.2% of wages and the employee pays 6.2% — 12.4% combined — on the first $184,500 of wages per employee in 2026. Top employer cost: $11,439.00. The wage base itself is $184,500 (see Table 3 for history); the totals reflect the employer share only.
  • Medicare — 1.45% each, no limit. The employer pays 1.45% and withholds 1.45% from the employee on all wages — no wage-base limit. This is the only federal payroll tax that reaches every dollar of high-salary pay as well as the first dollar of low wages.
  • Additional Medicare — 0.9% (employee only, withheld above $200,000). It does not add to the employer's cost. For an employee earning $250,000, the employer withholds 0.9% on the $50,000 above $200,000 = $450.00, and does not add a matching contribution. Combined, the employer pays $3,625.00 in Medicare tax (1.45% × $250,000). The employer withholds 1.45% + 0.9% from the employee, on the wages above $200,000. This is the only exception to the rule that the rates are the same for employer and employee.
  • FUTA — 6.0% gross, 0.6% net. FUTA is paid by the employer only. The tax is on the first $7,000 of wages per employee per year. With the full state tax credit of 5.4%, the net rate is 0.6% and the employer's net cost is $42.00 per employee per year; the gross amount, before credit, is $420.00. State unemployment tax credits apply only up to the first $7,000 of wages. The employer pays 0.6% on that — once earnings pass $7,000, FUTA stops for the year, which is why the total cost of employment rises only $42.00 from $1,000 to $7,000 (the FUTA portion) and then levels off per employee.

Result: The total employer rate on the first $7,000 of wages is 8.25% (6.2% Social Security + 1.45% Medicare + 0.6% FUTA net). That is the rate the FUTA Calculator uses for its first $7,000 calculation, and it explains the shape of the curve in our companion study (see Employer Payroll Tax by Salary (2026)).

Additional Medicare tax — thresholds by filing status — 2026

Additional Medicare tax (0.9%) is withheld from the employee's wages above a threshold that depends on filing status. The employer never pays it and never matches it. The employer still has to withhold it — even if the employer does not know the employee's filing status, because the withholding threshold uses wages, not household income.

Filing statusThreshold for Additional Medicare (2026)What the employer withholds
Married filing jointly$250,0000.9% on wages above $250,000
Married filing separately$125,0000.9% on wages above $125,000
Single$200,0000.9% on wages above $200,000
Head of household$200,0000.9% on wages above $200,000
Qualifying surviving spouse$200,0000.9% on wages above $200,000

Note: Additional Medicare tax applies only to wages above $200,000 for all filing statuses, except married filing jointly ($250,000) and married filing separately ($125,000). The withholding threshold means that when an employer's total wages to an employee pass $200,000, they must start withholding 0.9% on the excess — even if the employee's total household income would be higher or lower than the threshold. Withholding is only one piece of the picture — the actual tax depends on the household's total Medicare wages (combined wages for a couple filing jointly), which may be more or less than the employer's withholding. The entire paragraph above concerns the employee's withholding, not the employer's cost.

Sources: IRS: Additional Medicare tax; IRS: Additional Medicare tax (individuals); IRS Publication 15.

Example — $250,000 salary, single, no other compensation

A single filer — the withholding threshold is $200,000, so the employer withholds 0.9% on the $50,000 above $200,000 = $450.00, and does not match it. The employer's own cost for that $250,000 employee is Social Security $11,439.00 + Medicare $3,625.00 + FUTA $42.00 = $15,106.00.

Social Security wage base — how the ceiling has moved (2016–2026)

The wage base rose every year in the period except 2016, when it was unchanged from 2015. The largest single-year rise was 9.0% in 2023; the 2026 rise was 4.8% ($176,100 → $184,500). The employer Social Security cost at the cap has risen from $7,347.00 in 2016 to $11,439.00 in 2026 — a 55.7% increase over the decade.

Looking ahead — the 2027 wage base

As of the first publication of this page (November 11, 2026), the SSA had publicly projected — but not yet announced — a 2027 wage base of $192,300. That would be +4.2% from 2026, with a maximum employer Social Security of $11,922.60. The official 2027 number will be announced in autumn 2026; the value here is a published projection (SSA) for planning, not a confirmed 2027 figure. This page will be updated when the official 2027 wage base is announced; the number to use for 2027 payroll — the official figure — is the SSA wage base announced by the SSA.

Deposit schedules — when the money must move

The filing date is one event, the tax deposit is another. Most employers make either monthly or semi-weekly deposits — the schedule depends on how much tax the employer owed in a lookback period — and deposits are strictly separate from the quarterly filing.

ScheduleWho uses itDeposit window
MonthlyEmployers who owed $50,000 or less in the lookback periodBy the 15th day of the following month
SemiweeklyEmployers who owed more than $50,000 in the lookback periodPaydays on Wednesday, Thursday or Friday: by the following Wednesday. Paydays on Saturday, Sunday, Monday or Tuesday: by the following Friday.
Next-day deposit / $100,000+ ruleAny employer whose accumulated employment taxes reach $100,000, regardless of scheduleThe next business day

Note: Your deposit schedule is determined by your total tax liabilities for the lookback period, which is the 12 months ending on June 30, 2025 for the 2026 tax year. New employers — those who have not filed before — are monthly schedule depositors by default. See IRS: Depositing and reporting employment taxes.

Reading the examples

  • The employer's cost rises fastest at the bottom. The effective rate is highest at low salaries because the FUTA $42.00 is spread over a small salary: $42.00 is 0.42% of $10,000, 0.21% of $20,000 and 0.08% of $50,000.
  • Once Social Security caps, the rate falls. At $184,500 and above, Social Security is fixed at $11,439.00; only Medicare grows with pay, so the effective rate drops from 7.67% at $184,500 to 7.19% at $200,000 and 3.75% at $500,000 (see the CSV for the intermediate salaries).
  • Nothing here is a tax on the employer's profit or income. These are payroll taxes on wages — the employer pays them whether the business is profitable or not. They are a cost of employing, not a tax on total company income.

Methodology — how these numbers were produced

  • Rates come directly from the IRS and SSA publications cited in the footnotes. All figures use the 2026 tax year: the Social Security wage base ($184,500), the Medicare rate (1.45% each, uncapped), the Additional Medicare withholding thresholds ($200,000 / $250,000 / $125,000), and the FUTA gross rate (6.0% on the first $7,000, with a 5.4% credit).
  • Totals in Tables 1 and 5 are employer-side only. The employee's withholding is shown where it matters (Additional Medicare) but is never included in the employer totals.
  • FUTA is calculated at the net rate of 0.6% — the 6.0% gross rate minus the full 5.4% credit for timely state contributions. This is the same method used in the companion salary study, so the numbers here are consistent with the numbers there.
  • The wage-base history in Table 3 uses the SSA's published annual contribution and benefit base; the change column is the annual change in the wage base.
  • Deadline dates use the IRS's published due dates, with the weekend rule applied: when the statutory due date falls on a Saturday, Sunday or legal holiday (or the deposit falls on a non-business day, such as the second Sunday of a month), the date is the next business day.
  • Verified: November 11, 2026 — the IRS and SSA pages cited below were accessible and showed the figures used here.

Limitations and exclusions

  • This page is not a substitute for professional tax advice. It covers only federal payroll taxes; income-tax withholding uses the employee's Form W-4 and is not shown here.
  • State payroll taxes are excluded — every employer adds state unemployment insurance (SUI), and sometimes state income tax, disability and paid-family-leave taxes, on top of the federal numbers here.
  • Other common items are excluded: company health insurance, employer retirement-plan contributions, and other fringe benefits are not part of the federal payroll tax and do not appear in these tables.
  • FUTA credits assume the employer's state is not a credit-reduction state in 2026. In credit-reduction states the employer's FUTA rate is higher. Those states are not listed in this page; the IRS maintains the authoritative list.
  • Deadlines can be altered by law or by IRS relief — for disaster relief, for example, the IRS can postpone deposit and filing dates for affected areas.
  • All dollar amounts in this document are in the local currency as published by the IRS and SSA (U.S. dollars).
