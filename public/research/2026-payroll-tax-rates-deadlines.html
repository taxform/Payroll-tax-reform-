2026 U.S. Payroll Tax Rates, Wage Bases & Deadlines — Complete Reference for Employers

By PayrollTaxReform.com · Updated November 11, 2026 · Educational information — not tax advice · Sources: IRS & SSA — details at footnotes

One page with the federal payroll-tax numbers employers actually use: the 2026 Social Security wage base ($184,500), the 2026 FUTA net rate (0.6%), the Medicare thresholds, and the key filing and deposit deadlines — pulled from the primary IRS and SSA publications.

Who this is for

This is a one-page reference for U.S. employers, payroll managers, accountants, and HR teams who need the 2026 federal payroll tax numbers in one place — the rates, the wage caps, and the filing and deposit deadlines — with every figure traceable to the IRS or the Social Security Administration (SSA).

If you only need one number, it is in the tables below. If you need the full employer cost at a given salary, see “Worked employer-cost examples” and the companion study, Employer Payroll Tax by Salary (2026), which gives the federal employer cost for 25 salary levels from $20,000 to $500,000.

A note on timing

This reference was verified on November 11, 2026. The Q1–Q3 2026 Form 941 deadlines have already passed; the deadlines still ahead are the Q4 2026 Form 941, the 2026 Form 940, and the 2026 Forms W-2/W-3, all due February 1, 2027 (see the deadlines table).

Key findings

A $50,000 employee costs the employer $3,867 in federal payroll taxes in 2026 — 7.73% on top of salary, before state taxes and benefits.The total is Social Security (6.2% of $50,000 = $3,100) + Medicare (1.45% of $50,000 = $725) + FUTA net (0.6% of $7,000 = $42). The Additional Medicare tax is employee-only and does not change the employer cost.
The employer's effective federal rate falls as pay rises: 7.86% on a $20,000 salary, 7.67% at the $184,500 Social Security wage base, and 3.75% on a $500,000 salary — because Social Security stops at the wage base while Medicare does not.At $184,500 Social Security caps; above that only Medicare (1.45%) still applies to every dollar.
The Social Security wage base rose 4.8% in 2026, from $176,100 to $184,500.The most an employer can owe in Social Security tax for one employee rose $520.80, to $11,439.00.
The rates themselves did not change in 2026.Social Security is still 6.2% each side and Medicare is still 1.45% each side; what moves each year is the wage base.

What each line means — and where the numbers come from

  • Social Security, 6.2% each side (12.4% total). Both the employer and the employee pay 6.2% on wages up to $184,500. Once an employee's year-to-date wages pass $184,500, Social Security withholding stops for the rest of the calendar year — for both sides — and restarts on January 1.
  • Medicare, 1.45% each side (2.9% total). There is no wage base limit for Medicare tax; all covered wages are subject to it.
  • Additional Medicare Tax, 0.9%, employee only. The employer must withhold an extra 0.9% from wages paid to an employee in excess of $200,000 in a calendar year, starting in the pay period in which wages first exceed $200,000 and continuing until year-end. There is no employer share. The employer's $200,000 withholding threshold applies without regard to filing status.
  • FUTA, 6.0% gross / 0.6% net. FUTA is 6.0% on the first $7,000 of each employee's annual wages. Employers can generally take a credit of up to 5.4% for amounts paid into state unemployment funds, leaving a net rate of 0.6% — $42.00 per employee per year. Employers in a FUTA credit-reduction state receive a smaller credit and pay more; the U.S. Department of Labor announces credit-reduction states each year, and the reduction is figured on Schedule A (Form 940). Do not withhold FUTA from employee wages.

Example — a married employee filing jointly who earns $220,000

A married employee filing jointly who earns $220,000 has $180.00 withheld (0.9% × the $20,000 above $200,000). The employee's own liability threshold is $250,000, so no Additional Medicare Tax is ultimately owed on $220,000 alone — the $180 withheld is reconciled on Form 8959. Conversely, a married employee filing separately who earns $150,000 has nothing withheld (under the $200,000 employer threshold) but owes 0.9% on the $25,000 above the $125,000 threshold. Employers should not try to adjust withholding for an employee's filing status; the $200,000 rule is the employer's rule.

Reading the examples

  • $60,000 salary: the employer pays $4,632.00 in federal payroll taxes. That is the figure behind the site's True Cost of an Employee article, which adds state unemployment to reach $64,772–$67,032 in mandatory payroll taxes alone. A $60,000 salary costs about $4,600–$7,000 a year in payroll taxes before benefits.
  • $184,500 — the wage-base row: this is the last salary at which the full 6.2% Social Security match applies. Above it, only Medicare continues.
  • $250,000 salary: the employee also has 0.9% Additional Medicare Tax withheld on the $50,000 above $200,000 — $450.00, paid by the employee, not matched by the employer. At $500,000 the employee's Additional Medicare withholding is $2,700.00 (0.9% × $300,000).
  • Why the effective rate falls: at $20,000 the employer rate is 7.86%; at $500,000 it is 3.75%. The $42 FUTA charge and the capped Social Security rate are what push the percentage down at higher salaries — the rate falls because the tax base stops growing, not because the rate changes. Methodology

  • Scope: federal payroll taxes only — Social Security, Medicare, Additional Medicare Tax, and FUTA — for wage payments in calendar year 2026.
  • Calculation: for each salary, Social Security = 6.2% × min(salary, $184,500); Medicare = 1.45% × salary; FUTA (net) = 0.6% × min(salary, $7,000).
  • Deadlines: standard due dates are the last day of the month after the quarter or year ends, moved to the next business day when the due date falls on a weekend or legal holiday. Where the IRS grants a 10-day extension for timely depositors, it is the 10th day of the second month after the return period ends — for example, the Form 941 for Q4 2025 was due January 31, 2026; with a full and timely deposit it was due February 10, 2026.
  • Verification: every rate, wage base, threshold, and deadline in this reference was checked against an IRS or SSA publication on November 11, 2026, and is cited below.
  • What this page does not do: it does not cover farm workers (Form 943), household employers (the $3,000 household-wage threshold is separate), or the 10-year lookahead in the SSA trustees' report.

Limitations

  • FUTA credit-reduction states pay more. The $42.00 FUTA figure assumes the full 5.4% credit. In a credit-reduction state the credit is smaller, the net FUTA rate is higher, and the employer's cost is more.
  • Figures change every year. The Social Security wage base is adjusted annually; the 2027 base is already published at $192,300.
  • Deposit rules have exceptions. The monthly/semiweekly summary above is the standard rule; the $100,000 next-day rule, the accuracy-of-deposits rule, and holiday provisions (Pub. 15, Circular E) apply.
  • This is not tax advice. Confirm figures with the IRS, SSA, your state agencies, and your payroll professional before filing or relying on them.
