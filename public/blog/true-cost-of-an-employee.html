The True Cost of an Employee: What a $60,000 Salary Really Costs

First-time employers routinely under-budget hires by 10% or more. Here is the line-by-line employer math — and a rule of thumb you can use for every offer letter.

Worked example: $60,000 salary

Employer costRateAnnual amount
Gross salary—$60,000.00
Social Security match6.2%$3,720.00
Medicare match1.45%$870.00
FUTA (with full credit)0.6% of first $7,000$42.00
State unemployment (SUTA)Varies; ~2–4% typical for new employers$140–$2,400+
Mandatory payroll taxes alone—$64,772–$67,032

That's before workers' compensation insurance, health benefits, retirement match, paid leave mandates, and payroll software fees. A common planning rule: budget 1.1× to 1.25× salary for the fully loaded cost of a hire, and refine from there.

Where the estimate swings most

Biggest swing

State unemployment rates

New-employer SUTA rates and wage bases differ enormously by state — from $7,000 to over $60,000 in taxable wages. Look yours up in the state guides.

Often missed

Workers' comp & paid leave

Workers' comp premiums depend on job classification, and a growing number of states mandate paid family leave or disability contributions. Price these before the offer.

High earners

The wage-base effect

Above the $176,100 Social Security cap, the 6.2% match disappears — so highly paid hires cost proportionally less in payroll tax. Model it in the payroll calculator.
