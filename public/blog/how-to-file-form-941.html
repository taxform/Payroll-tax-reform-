How to File Form 941 (2026): Line-by-Line Guide for Employers

Form 941, the Employer's Quarterly Federal Tax Return, is the form most U.S. employers file four times a year. It is where you report the federal income tax you withheld from your employees' pay, plus both halves of Social Security and Medicare tax, and reconcile those amounts against the deposits you already made during the quarter. This guide walks through it line by line, with a worked example, the 2026 due dates, and the penalties for filing or depositing late.

Quick answer: File Form 941 once per quarter, due on the last day of the month after the quarter ends — April 30, July 31, November 2, and February 1 for the four 2026 quarters. If you deposited all of the quarter's taxes on time and in full, you get an automatic 10-day extension to file. Use the March 2026 revision of the form for all four 2026 quarters.

What Form 941 is — and what it is not

Form 941 reports three things for one calendar quarter:

  • Federal income tax withheld from employees' wages, tips, and other compensation.
  • Social Security tax — 6.2% from the employee and a matching 6.2% from the employer, on wages up to the 2026 wage base of $184,500 per employee.
  • Medicare tax — 1.45% from the employee and a matching 1.45% from the employer, on all wages, with no wage cap. Plus the 0.9% Additional Medicare Tax withheld from employees on wages over $200,000 (employee only — there is no employer share).

Form 941 does not report federal unemployment tax — that is Form 940, filed annually. It does not report backup withholding or withholding on nonpayroll payments such as pensions — that is Form 945. And it does not cover agricultural labor (Form 943) or household employees (usually Schedule H). If you are not sure which form applies, the Instructions for Form 941, not a vendor blog, are the authority.

One point employers get wrong: filing and depositing are separate steps. Form 941 is the quarterly report. The tax money itself is deposited during the quarter, on a monthly or semiweekly schedule, through EFTPS or another electronic method. By the time you file, the balance due on the return should usually be zero — the deposits have already paid it. If it is not zero, something went wrong during the quarter, and the return is where it shows up.

Who must file Form 941

File Form 941 if you paid wages subject to Social Security and Medicare taxes or subject to federal income tax withholding. File your first return for the quarter in which you first paid such wages, then file every quarter after that, even if you paid no wages in a quarter, unless you filed a final return or one of the exceptions below applies.

Exceptions:

  • Form 944 filers. If the IRS has notified you in writing to file Form 944, the annual return, file that instead — do not file Form 941. Form 944 is only for the smallest employers, generally those whose annual employment tax liability is $1,000 or less. You cannot choose it yourself; the IRS assigns it. If you want to switch between 941 and 944, you must request the change from the IRS and receive written notice before you file the other form.
  • Seasonal employers. You do not have to file for quarters in which you paid no wages and have no tax liability. Check the box on line 18 on every return you do file, to tell the IRS you will not be filing for one or more quarters.
  • Household employers do not usually file Form 941; farm employers do not file it for agricultural labor.
  • Closing the business. If you permanently stop paying wages, file a final return: check the box on line 17, enter the final date you paid wages, and attach a statement showing who is keeping the payroll records and where they are kept.

Use the March 2026 revision of Form 941 for 2026. Do not use an earlier revision to report 2026 taxes — the IRS says it expects the March 2026 revision and its instructions to cover all four 2026 quarters.

2026 due dates

Form 941 is due by the last day of the month that follows the end of the quarter. If that day falls on a Saturday, Sunday, or legal holiday, the due date moves to the next business day.

The 10-day extension is automatic — there is no form to file for it — but it only applies if you deposited all of the quarter's taxes on time and in full. A paper return postmarked by the due date counts as filed on time, if it is properly addressed and has sufficient postage.

Form 941, line by line

Form 941 has five parts across two pages. You must complete both pages and sign on page 2. Enter your name and EIN on all pages, and do not use your Social Security number or ITIN where the form asks for an EIN.

Part 1 — Lines 1–4: the quarter's payroll in brief

LineWhat goes here
Line 1 — Number of employeesThe number of employees on your payroll for the pay period including March 12, June 12, September 12, or December 12, depending on the quarter. Do not include household employees, employees in nonpay status, farm employees, pensioners, or active members of the U.S. Armed Forces.
Line 2 — Wages, tips, and other compensationTotal wages, tips, and other compensation for the quarter — the same amounts that go in box 1 of your employees' Forms W-2. Enter the amount before payroll deductions.
Line 3 — Federal income tax withheldThe federal income tax you withheld (or were required to withhold) from this quarter's wages, tips, taxable fringe benefits, and supplemental unemployment compensation benefits.
Line 4 — No wages subject to Social Security or Medicare taxCheck this box only if none of the wages on line 2 are subject to Social Security or Medicare tax. Otherwise leave it blank.

Part 1 — Lines 5a–5f: Social Security and Medicare taxes

This is the heart of the form. Column 1 is the taxable wages; column 2 is the tax, calculated at the combined employer-plus-employee rate.

LineColumn 1 (wages)Column 2 (tax)
5a — Taxable Social Security wagesAll wages, sick pay, and taxable fringe benefits subject to Social Security tax, up to $184,500 per employee for 2026. Do not include tips on this line. Stop entering an employee's wages here once their year-to-date taxable wages reach $184,500 — but keep withholding income and Medicare taxes on all wages for the whole year.Column 1 × 0.124 (6.2% employee + 6.2% employer)
5b — Taxable Social Security tipsAll tips your employees reported to you during the quarter, until the employee's wages plus tips reach $184,500 for the year. Employees must report cash tips to you by the 10th of the month after the month they received them; no report is required for months when tips are under $20.Column 1 × 0.124
5c — Taxable Medicare wages & tipsAll wages, tips, sick pay, and taxable fringe benefits subject to Medicare tax. There is no wage limit for Medicare.Column 1 × 0.029 (1.45% + 1.45%)
5d — Wages subject to Additional Medicare Tax withholdingWages subject to Additional Medicare Tax withholding. You must begin withholding the extra 0.9% in the pay period in which you pay an employee wages over $200,000 in the calendar year, and keep withholding it until year-end. It is employee-only — there is no employer share.Column 1 × 0.009
5e — Total Social Security and Medicare taxesAdd the column 2 amounts on lines 5a–5d.
5f — Section 3121(q) Notice and DemandTax due on unreported tips, only if the IRS has issued you a Section 3121(q) Notice and Demand. Most employers leave this blank.

Part 1 — Lines 6–16: totals, adjustments, and what you owe

LineWhat goes here
Line 6 — Total taxes before adjustmentsLine 3 + line 5e + line 5f.
Lines 7–9 — Current-quarter adjustmentsLine 7: fractions-of-cents rounding adjustments on the employee share. Line 8: sick-pay adjustments where a third-party payer transferred the employer share of Social Security and Medicare taxes to you. Line 9: uncollected employee share of Social Security and Medicare taxes on tips and on group-term life insurance for former employees (a negative adjustment). Do not use lines 7–9 to fix a prior quarter — that is Form 941-X (see below).
Line 10 — Total taxes after adjustmentsLines 6–9 combined.
Line 11 — Qualified small business payroll tax creditOnly if you elected the research credit on Form 6765 and are reporting it on Form 8974 — enter the amount from Form 8974 and attach Form 8974. Most employers leave this blank.
Line 12 — Total taxes after adjustments and nonrefundable creditsLine 10 minus line 11. This is the quarter's total tax liability, and it must equal the total liability on line 16 or on Schedule B (see below).
Line 13 — Total deposits for this quarterYour deposits for the quarter, including any overpayment from a prior quarter that you applied to this return.
Line 14 — Balance dueIf line 12 is more than line 13, the difference. Never enter amounts on both line 14 and line 15a. You do not have to pay if line 14 is under $1. If you deposited everything on time, line 14 should be zero.
Line 15a–15e — OverpaymentIf line 13 is more than line 12, the difference goes on 15a. On 15b choose one: have the overpayment refunded or applied to your next return. If you choose a refund, complete lines 15c–15e for direct deposit — the IRS now issues Form 941 refunds by direct deposit.
Line 16 — Tax liability for the quarterCheck one box. If line 12 (or the prior quarter's line 12) is under $2,500 and you had no $100,000 next-day deposit obligation, check the first box and go to Part 3. Monthly schedule depositors check the second box and enter the tax liability for each month of the quarter. Semiweekly schedule depositors check the third box and attach Schedule B (Form 941), the daily record of tax liability. Line 16 is a record of your liability by month or day — not a list of the deposits you made.

Part 2 — Deposit schedule (line 16 / Schedule B)

Your deposit schedule comes from your lookback period — the 12 months from July 1 of the second preceding year through June 30 of the prior year. For 2026, that is July 1, 2024, through June 30, 2025.

  • Monthly depositor — you reported $50,000 or less in employment taxes in the lookback period. Deposit taxes on wages paid in a given month by the 15th of the following month. New employers start as monthly depositors.
  • Semiweekly depositor — you reported more than $50,000 in the lookback period. Paydays Wednesday–Friday: deposit by the following Wednesday. Paydays Saturday–Tuesday: deposit by the following Friday.
  • $100,000 next-day rule — if you accumulate $100,000 or more in taxes on any day during a deposit period, deposit by the next business day, whatever your schedule. You then become a semiweekly depositor for the rest of that year and the next.
  • Under $2,500 — if your total taxes for the current or prior quarter are under $2,500 and you had no $100,000 next-day obligation, you may pay with your timely filed return instead of depositing during the quarter.

All federal tax deposits must be made electronically — through EFTPS, your IRS business tax account, or IRS Direct Pay. EFTPS deposits must be scheduled by 8 p.m. Eastern time the day before the deposit is due. See IRS Topic No. 757 for the full deposit rules.

Parts 3–5 — Business information, third-party designee, and signature

Part 3 covers whether your business closed or stopped paying wages (line 17, final return), and whether you are a seasonal employer (line 18). Part 4 is the optional third-party designee — someone the IRS may discuss the return with. Part 5 is the signature: complete both pages and sign on page 2, or processing may be delayed. If you use a paid preparer, the preparer signs too.

Worked example: a 3-employee quarter

A small business has 3 employees. In Q4 2026 each is paid $15,000 in wages, for $45,000 total. Federal income tax withheld from their pay for the quarter is $4,500. No employee is anywhere near the $184,500 Social Security wage base or the $200,000 Additional Medicare threshold, and there are no adjustments or credits. Here is how the return fills in:

LineFigureHow it is calculated
Line 1 — Number of employees3Employees on payroll in the pay period including December 12
Line 2 — Wages, tips, and other compensation$45,000.003 × $15,000
Line 3 — Federal income tax withheld$4,500.00As withheld from pay (illustrative figure)
Line 5a — Taxable Social Security wages$45,000.00 × 0.124 = $5,580.006.2% employee + 6.2% employer
Line 5c — Taxable Medicare wages & tips$45,000.00 × 0.029 = $1,305.001.45% employee + 1.45% employer
Line 5e — Total Social Security and Medicare taxes$6,885.00$5,580.00 + $1,305.00
Line 6 — Total taxes before adjustments$11,385.00$4,500.00 + $6,885.00
Line 12 — Total taxes after adjustments and credits$11,385.00No adjustments or credits in this example
Line 13 — Total deposits for this quarter$11,385.00Deposited during the quarter on the monthly schedule
Line 14 — Balance due$0.00Line 12 minus line 13

Of the $6,885.00 in Social Security and Medicare taxes, the employer's share is $3,442.50 (6.2% of $45,000 = $2,790.00 Social Security, plus 1.45% of $45,000 = $652.50 Medicare) and the employees' share, withheld from their pay, is the same $3,442.50. Check your own return against this pattern: line 12 must equal the total liability on line 16 (or Schedule B). If it does not, your deposits may not be counted as timely — fix the liability record before you file, rather than adjusting the deposits to match.

How to file Form 941

E-file (recommended). The IRS accepts Form 941 electronically through Modernized e-File, either through payroll software or an authorized e-file provider. File once per quarter — if you filed electronically, do not also file a paper return. Form 941-X can also be e-filed.

Paper. Mail the completed form to the IRS address in the instructions for your state — the "Where Should You File" section of the Instructions for Form 941 lists separate addresses for returns with and without a payment. If you pay a balance due by check or money order, make it payable to "United States Treasury," write your EIN, "Form 941," and the tax period on it, and enclose Form 941-V, the payment voucher. Do not send Form 941-V if you pay electronically.

Paying a balance due. If you were required to make deposits, pay any balance due electronically. If you were not required to deposit (the under-$2,500 rule), you may pay by EFT, credit or debit card, check, money order, or electronic funds withdrawal. If you cannot pay in full, the IRS offers an online installment agreement if you owe $25,000 or less and can pay it off within 24 months — interest and penalties still apply to the unpaid amount.

Most employers do not file Form 941 by hand at all: full-service payroll software calculates, deposits, and files it for you. That is the main reason to compare providers on tax-filing coverage, not just price — see payroll software for small businesses. If you run payroll yourself, the two things that matter most are the deposit schedule (get it right during the quarter) and the line 16 / Schedule B liability record (get it right on the return).

Penalties for filing or depositing late

Three separate penalties can apply, and they are not the same thing:

PenaltyRate
Failure to file — the return is late5% of the unpaid tax for each month (or part of a month) the return is late, up to 25%. Filing on time even if you cannot pay in full avoids this larger penalty.
Failure to pay — tax shown on the return is unpaid0.5% of the unpaid tax per month, up to 25%. Interest also accrues on unpaid amounts.
Failure to deposit — a deposit is late or short2% if 1–5 days late; 5% if 6–15 days late; 10% if more than 15 days late; 15% if still unpaid more than 10 days after the first IRS notice (or on demand for immediate payment). The tiers replace each other — they do not stack.

Source: IRS Publication 15 (2026), Employer’s Tax Guide, and the IRS Failure to Deposit Penalty rules. The deposit penalty is the one small employers hit most often, because it is triggered by a single missed deposit date during the quarter — not by the filing date. A semiweekly depositor who reports liabilities on line 16 instead of Schedule B can also be assessed an "averaged" failure-to-deposit penalty, so use the right schedule for your deposit type.

There is also the trust fund recovery penalty: the withheld employee taxes are trust fund taxes, held for the government. If they are not collected or paid over, the IRS can assess a penalty equal to 100% of the unpaid trust fund taxes personally against the people responsible for collecting and paying them who acted willfully. That is why the deposit schedule matters more than the filing date.

Form 941 vs. Form 944 — which one do you file?

Most employers file Form 941 quarterly. Form 944 is the annual version for the smallest employers — generally those whose annual employment tax liability (Social Security, Medicare, and withheld income tax combined) is $1,000 or less, which the IRS says usually means about $5,000 or less in wages for the year. You file Form 944 only if the IRS has notified you in writing to do so. If you have not received that notice, you file Form 941. The two forms report the same taxes; only the frequency differs. Form 944 for 2026 is due February 1, 2027.

Correcting a mistake: Form 941-X

Do not file a second Form 941 for the same quarter, and do not fix a prior quarter's error on the current quarter's return. Use Form 941-X, Adjusted Employer's Quarterly Federal Tax Return or Claim for Refund — one form per quarter, filed separately from Form 941.

  • Underreported tax: file Form 941-X by the due date of the return for the quarter in which you discovered the error, and pay what you owe when you file. That generally keeps the correction interest-free and free of failure-to-pay and failure-to-deposit penalties.
  • Overreported tax: file Form 941-X to claim a credit on a later return or a refund. You generally have 3 years from the date the Form 941 was filed (or 2 years from when you paid the tax, if later) to correct overreported tax.
  • Income tax withholding errors: you can generally correct federal income tax withholding errors only if you discovered them in the same calendar year you paid the wages — and, for an overcollection, only if you also repaid or reimbursed the employees in that same year. You cannot correct a prior year's withholding on Form 941-X just because you used the wrong table.
  • What 941-X does not fix: errors only in the number of employees (line 1) or only in the tax liabilities reported on line 16 / Schedule B follow separate procedures.

5 mistakes that trigger IRS notices

  1. Depositing on the filing schedule instead of the deposit schedule. The return is quarterly; the money moves monthly or semiweekly. Waiting until the filing date to pay is a failure-to-deposit penalty.
  2. Line 12 does not equal line 16 / Schedule B. The return's total tax and the liability record must match, or deposits may not be counted as timely.
  3. Using the wrong quarter. Wages are reported in the quarter you paid them, not the quarter they were earned. Wages paid October 2 belong to Q4, even if the work was done in September.
  4. Stopping Social Security tax at the wrong wage base — or stopping Medicare too. Social Security stops at $184,500 per employee for 2026; Medicare does not stop at all. And the $200,000 Additional Medicare withholding threshold is per employee, per calendar year, regardless of filing status.
  5. Filing a second Form 941 to fix an error. Corrections go on Form 941-X, one per quarter. A duplicate 941 creates a second return for the same quarter and delays everything.

Frequently asked questions

Q: When is Form 941 due in 2026?
A: April 30, July 31, and November 2, 2026, and February 1, 2027 (for Q4 2026). If all deposits were made on time and in full, the filing deadline extends by 10 days: May 11, August 10, and November 10, 2026, and February 10, 2027.

Q: Do I have to file Form 941 if I had no employees this quarter?
A: Yes, once you have filed your first Form 941 you must file every quarter, even with no wages to report, unless you filed a final return or you are a seasonal employer with no wages in that quarter.

Q: What is the difference between Form 941 and Form 940?
A: Form 941 is the quarterly return for withheld income tax and Social Security/Medicare taxes. Form 940 is the annual return for FUTA, the federal unemployment tax — 6.0% on the first $7,000 of each employee's wages, 0.6% net with the full state credit. They are separate forms with separate due dates; Form 940 for 2026 is due February 1, 2027.

Q: Can I pay my Form 941 balance with the return?
A: Only if your total taxes for the current or prior quarter are under $2,500 and you had no $100,000 next-day deposit obligation. Otherwise you must deposit during the quarter, and any balance due on line 14 should be zero if you deposited everything on time.

Q: What if I cannot pay the full amount?
A: File on time anyway — the failure-to-file penalty (5% per month) is ten times the failure-to-pay penalty (0.5% per month). Then apply for an online installment agreement if you owe $25,000 or less and can pay within 24 months.

Q: Does payroll software file Form 941 for me?
A: Full-service plans do — they calculate, deposit, and file federal, state, and local payroll taxes, including Form 941. Self-service or calculate-only plans do not; you file it yourself. Check the plan, not just the provider name. See payroll software for small businesses.
