Texas Payroll Taxes 2026: Employer Guide (No State Income Tax — What You Still Owe)

Texas is the simplest large state for payroll — no state income tax, no state withholding form, and exactly one state payroll tax. But "simple" is not the same as "nothing to do": you still have to register with the Texas Workforce Commission, pay unemployment tax on the first $9,000 of each employee's wages, file quarterly wage reports, report new hires within 20 days, and pay final paychecks on the Texas Payday Law's clock. This guide covers all of it for 2026, with a worked example for a $60,000 employee.

Quick answer: Texas has no state income tax, so there is nothing to withhold for the state. The only state payroll tax is the unemployment tax (SUTA), paid entirely by the employer to the Texas Workforce Commission: 2.70% for new employers in 2026 on the first $9,000 of each employee's wages ($243 per employee per year at most). Register with the TWC within 10 days of becoming liable, file Form C-3 quarterly (April 30, July 31, October 31, January 31), and report new hires within 20 days.

Figures verified November 14, 2026 against the Texas Workforce Commission's published 2026 tax rates, new-employer information, liable-employer responsibilities, new-hire reporting, and final-pay pages, plus the IRS and Department of Labor FUTA credit-reduction guidance. The 2026 FUTA credit-reduction list was not final on that date — see the note in the federal section below.

That is the whole state layer: one tax, employer-paid, on a $9,000 wage base. Everything else on a Texas paycheck is federal. Compare that with California, where employers handle four state taxes — the Texas guide is shorter because there is genuinely less to do.

No state income tax — what that does and does not mean

Texas employers withhold nothing for the state: no state income tax from the paycheck, no state withholding form to collect (there is no Texas equivalent of California's DE 4), and no state withholding deposits. Federal income tax withholding still applies, using the employee's W-4, and federal Social Security and Medicare withholding still apply.

What it does not mean:

  • It does not mean no state payroll tax. Texas unemployment tax is real, and new employers are the ones most likely to miss it — it is the tax most often forgotten by businesses moving into Texas.
  • It does not mean no state filings. You still register with the TWC, file quarterly wage reports, and report new hires to the Attorney General.
  • It does not change the federal side at all. Form 941 quarterly, Form 940 annually, and W-2s by February 1, 2027 all still apply — see our Form 941 guide and Form 940 guide.
  • It does not mean employees pay no state tax. Texas makes up the revenue elsewhere — notably sales tax, which is not a payroll matter but is why "no income tax" should never be quoted to an employee as "no Texas tax."

Texas unemployment tax (SUTA): the only state payroll tax

Texas unemployment insurance is paid entirely by the employer — it never comes out of an employee's paycheck, and you cannot deduct it from wages. It is administered by the Texas Workforce Commission (TWC) under the Texas Unemployment Compensation Act (TUCA), and it funds unemployment benefits for workers who lose their jobs through no fault of their own.

  • 2026 new-employer rate: 2.70%. Newly liable employers are assigned an entry-level rate equal to the higher of their industry (NAICS) average or 2.70%. For 2026 the entry-level rate is a flat 2.70% for all industry groups with no exceptions. You keep that rate until you have completed four chargeable quarters, after which the TWC assigns an interim rate based on your actual experience.
  • 2026 experienced-employer range: 0.32% to 6.32%, with the average experienced employer paying 0.99%. Your rate is the sum of five components set annually by the TWC: the General Tax Rate, the Replenishment Tax Rate (0.21% for 2026), the Obligation Assessment, the Deficit Tax Rate, and the Employment and Training Investment Assessment. The TWC mails your annual tax rate notice each December.
  • Wage base: the first $9,000 per employee per calendar year. Only wages up to that ceiling count — anything above it is not subject to state unemployment tax. The $9,000 figure has not changed since 1997, which keeps Texas per-employee costs low: at the new-employer rate the maximum is $243 a year; even at the top 6.32% rate it is $568.80.
  • Why the rate moves: the General Tax Rate is driven by your own claims history — benefits paid to your former employees over the last three years, divided by your taxable wages. Fewer claims means a lower rate next year, which is the one lever you actually control: clean separations and accurate wage reports keep claims (and your rate) down.

Because the wage base is only $9,000, Texas SUTA is a small line item for most employers — the same $9,000 base as Florida and California's $7,000, and far below states like Washington ($72,800). The expensive part of a Texas payroll is federal Social Security and Medicare, not the state.

Registering as a Texas employer

Register with the TWC within 10 days of paying an employee wage and becoming liable for unemployment tax, through the TWC's Unemployment Tax Registration system. You receive a TWC account number, which is your official identifier for all future filings.

  • When are you liable? Under TUCA you are generally a liable employer if you pay $1,500 or more in wages during a calendar quarter, or you employ one or more people for at least part of a day in 20 different weeks within a calendar year. Either test triggers liability — check both, not just the one you remember.
  • Before your first payroll: get your federal EIN, register with the TWC, collect a W-4 and I-9 from each employee, and confirm your rate (2.70% if you are new). Our first-payroll checklist walks through the federal side.
  • New hires are a separate step: reporting a new hire to the Attorney General is not the same as registering with the TWC, and doing one does not satisfy the other — see below.

Filing and paying: Form C-3, quarterly

Texas employers file Form C-3 (Employer's Quarterly Report) with Form C-4 (Wages List) — the quarterly totals plus the employee-by-employee wage detail — and pay the unemployment tax for the quarter. A return must be filed even if no wages were paid during the reporting period.

The deadline is the last day of the month following the end of the quarter. Electronic filing and payment are mandatory for all employers under TWC Rules 815.107 and 815.109 — file through the TWC's Unemployment Tax Services (UTS) portal, which handles wage reports for up to 1,000 employees and tax payments. Paper filing is available only with an approved hardship waiver. Check the TWC's due-dates calendar for weekend and holiday adjustments.

These are the state dates. The federal dates run alongside them: Form 941 quarterly, Form 940 annually (due February 1, 2027), and W-2s to employees by February 1, 2027. New hire reporting: 20 calendar days

Federal and state law require employers to report all new hires and rehires to the Texas Office of the Attorney General (Employer New Hire Reporting Operations Center) within 20 calendar days of the hire date. If you report electronically, you must report 12 to 16 days apart — about twice a month. The rule of thumb: if the employee has to fill out a W-4, you have to report the hire.

  • Penalties: $25 for each employee an employer knowingly fails to report, and $500 for conspiring with an employee to fail to file a report or submitting a false or incomplete report.
  • Separate from TWC reporting. New-hire reporting to the Attorney General is a different filing from the quarterly wage report to the TWC — one does not cover the other.

The federal layer: 941, 940, W-2 — and no credit-reduction problem

Texas payroll is federal payroll plus the one state tax. You still file Form 941 quarterly, Form 940 annually, and W-2s by February 1, 2027 — see our Form 941 guide and Form 940 guide.

Good news on FUTA: Texas is not on the Department of Labor's list of potential 2026 FUTA credit-reduction states — only California and the U.S. Virgin Islands are. Texas employers who pay their state unemployment tax on time keep the full 5.4% credit, so the effective FUTA rate stays at 0.6% ($42 per employee per year). The final 2026 list is set by the DOL's November 10 determination and published by the IRS on Schedule A (Form 940); it was not final when this guide was drafted on November 14, 2026, but Texas is not a candidate for it, so the $42 figure is safe to plan on.

Minimum wage and the Texas Payday Law

Texas follows the federal floor on pay: the minimum wage is the federal $7.25 per hour, with a $2.13 cash wage for tipped employees, and no state overtime rule beyond the federal 40-hour week. There is no state-mandated paid sick leave or state disability program to withhold for.

Where Texas does have its own rules is when you pay. Under the Texas Payday Law (Labor Code Chapter 61), enforced by the TWC:

  • Discharged employee: final pay in full no later than the sixth calendar day after the discharge. The clock runs from the effective date of discharge, not the last day physically worked.
  • Employee who quits: final pay by the next regularly scheduled payday — unless the employee gave at least 72 hours' advance notice, in which case it is due within six calendar days of the last day of work.
  • Wage claims: an employee who is not paid on time can file a wage claim with the TWC within 180 days after the wages became due. If the TWC finds the employer acted in bad faith, it may assess an administrative penalty up to the lesser of the wages in question or $1,000.

Put the final-pay rule on the same checklist as the tax calendar: a missed six-day deadline is the most common Texas-specific payroll mistake, and it has nothing to do with tax rates.

5 mistakes that trigger TWC notices

  1. Never registering. The most common Texas mistake: an out-of-state business hires its first Texas employee, runs federal payroll correctly, and never registers with the TWC. Registration is due within 10 days of becoming liable — not when you get around to it, and not when the first quarterly report comes due.
  2. Applying SUTA to all wages. Texas unemployment tax stops at $9,000 per employee per year. Taxing a $60,000 salary at 2.70% overstates the tax by $1,377 per employee — and the C-3 will not reconcile.
  3. Forgetting the no-wages return. A C-3 must be filed even for a quarter with no wages paid. A missing quarter reads as a missing return, not as a zero return.
  4. Missing the 20-day new-hire window. New-hire reporting goes to the Attorney General, not the TWC, so it is easy to file the wage report and forget the hire report entirely. Calendar it from the hire date, not the first payday.
  5. Paying the final check on the next payroll run. A discharged employee's final pay is due within six calendar days, not on the next scheduled payday. Running it with the next regular payroll is late by definition if that payday falls after day six.

Frequently asked questions

Q: Does Texas have state income tax withholding?
A: No. Texas has no state income tax, so employers withhold nothing for the state — only federal income tax, Social Security, and Medicare come out of an employee's paycheck. The only state-level payroll tax in Texas is the unemployment tax, paid entirely by the employer to the Texas Workforce Commission.

Q: What is the Texas unemployment tax rate for a new employer in 2026?
A: A flat 2.70% entry-level rate in 2026, on the first $9,000 of each employee's wages — a maximum of $243 per employee per year. The rate is the higher of the industry average or 2.70% under the Texas Unemployment Compensation Act, and for 2026 it is 2.70% for all industry groups with no exceptions.

Q: What is the Texas SUTA wage base for 2026?
A: The first $9,000 of wages paid to each employee per calendar year. Once an employee's cumulative wages for the year pass $9,000, no additional state unemployment tax is due on that employee's remaining pay. The $9,000 wage base has not changed since 1997.

Q: When are Texas unemployment tax reports due?
A: Quarterly wage reports (Form C-3) and payments are due April 30, July 31, October 31, and January 31 — the last day of the month following the end of the quarter. A return must be filed even if no wages were paid, and all employers must file and pay electronically through the TWC's Unemployment Tax Services portal.

Q: How do I register as a Texas employer for unemployment tax?
A: Register with the Texas Workforce Commission within 10 days of paying wages and becoming liable for unemployment tax, through the TWC's Unemployment Tax Registration system. You are generally liable if you pay $1,500 or more in wages in a calendar quarter or employ one or more people for 20 different weeks in a year.

Q: How quickly must Texas employers report new hires?
A: Within 20 calendar days of the hire date, to the Texas Office of the Attorney General. This is separate from reporting quarterly wages to the Texas Workforce Commission. The penalty is $25 per employee knowingly not reported, and $500 for conspiring to file a false or incomplete report.

Q: When is a final paycheck due in Texas?
A: An employee who is discharged must be paid in full no later than the sixth calendar day after the discharge. An employee who quits is paid by the next regularly scheduled payday — unless they gave at least 72 hours' advance notice, in which case final pay is due within six calendar days of the last day of work.

Q: Is Texas a FUTA credit-reduction state for 2026?
A: No. Texas is not on the Department of Labor's list of potential 2026 FUTA credit-reduction states — only California and the U.S. Virgin Islands are. Texas employers who pay their state unemployment tax on time keep the full 5.4% FUTA credit, so the effective federal unemployment tax stays at 0.6%, or $42 per employee per year.
