W-2 vs 1099: The Misclassification Mistake That Costs Employers Everything

Skipping payroll taxes by labeling workers "contractors" is one of the most audited shortcuts in employment tax. Here's how the IRS actually decides — and what getting it wrong costs.

The label on the contract doesn't decide

The IRS evaluates three buckets of evidence — behavioral control (who directs how and when the work is done), financial control (who provides tools, bears expenses, and controls profit or loss), and the relationship itself (benefits, permanence, whether the work is core to the business). A worker you schedule, supervise, and supply with equipment will usually be an employee no matter what the paperwork says. When in doubt, employers can request an official determination with Form SS-8.

What misclassification actually costs

Federal

Back employment taxes + interest

Retroactive employer FICA on all misclassified wages, plus liability for income tax that should have been withheld — with interest running from each original due date.

Penalties

Stacking failure penalties

Failure to file, failure to deposit, and accuracy-related penalties can compound on top of the tax itself — and responsible officers can be held personally liable under IRC § 6672.

State

State agencies pile on

State labor departments assess unpaid unemployment insurance, workers' comp premiums, overtime and back wages — often triggered automatically by a single worker complaint or audit.

The safe comparison:

A W-2 hire at $60,000 costs roughly $65,000–$67,000 in mandatory payroll taxes (see the full math). A misclassified worker "saves" that amount right up until an audit multiplies it.
