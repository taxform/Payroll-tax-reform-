What Is SUTA Tax? State Unemployment Insurance Explained (2026)

SUTA — the State Unemployment Tax Act, also called state unemployment insurance (SUI) — is the state tax employers pay to fund unemployment benefits for workers who lose their jobs through no fault of their own. It is the state half of the unemployment system: FUTA is the small federal tax on top of it, and paying SUTA on time is what earns you the federal credit that keeps FUTA at $42 per employee per year. This guide explains what SUTA is, how your rate is set, what the wage base means, and how it connects to FUTA — with a worked example.

Quick answer: SUTA is the state unemployment tax employers pay to their state government to fund unemployment benefits. Each state runs its own program and sets its own tax rate and wage base, so the same employee costs a different amount of SUTA in different states. In almost every state the employer pays it entirely — only Alaska, New Jersey, and Pennsylvania also require a small employee contribution. SUTA is filed and paid quarterly to your state unemployment agency, and paying it on time and in full earns a credit of up to 5.4% against the federal FUTA tax.

What SUTA is — and where it sits in payroll tax

Unemployment insurance in the United States is a federal-state partnership. The federal government sets the framework and collects FUTA, the Federal Unemployment Tax Act tax. The states run the actual unemployment programs — they decide who qualifies for benefits, how much benefits pay, and how much employers contribute. SUTA is the state side of that partnership: the tax each state levies on employers to fill its own unemployment trust fund.

Three facts define SUTA:

  • It is state-run, so it varies. There is no single SUTA rate. Each state sets its own taxable wage base, its own rate schedule, and its own rules for new employers. An employer with identical payrolls in Texas and New York pays different SUTA in each state — and different amounts again for employees who work in different states.
  • It is employer-paid. In nearly every state, SUTA comes entirely out of the employer's pocket. It is not withheld from employees' wages and does not appear on their W-2s. The three exceptions are Alaska, New Jersey, and Pennsylvania, which also require employees to contribute a small percentage.
  • It is quarterly. SUTA is reported and paid on your state's quarterly wage report — the same filing where you report each employee's wages for the quarter. The federal return, Form 940, is annual; the state return is not.

SUTA is not the same as state income tax withholding, and it is not the same as FUTA. Withholding is money taken out of the employee's pay and sent to the state; SUTA is the employer's own cost. FUTA is the federal unemployment tax, reported on Form 940 — and the two are linked, because paying SUTA on time is what earns the FUTA credit. That link is the reason SUTA matters even to employers who think of it as a small line item.

How SUTA is calculated: the wage base and your rate

Your SUTA bill each quarter is a simple multiplication: taxable wages × your SUTA rate. Both parts of that formula are set by your state, and both are worth understanding separately.

The taxable wage base

The wage base is the maximum amount of each employee's annual wages subject to SUTA. Once an employee's earnings pass the base, you stop owing SUTA on that worker for the rest of the calendar year. The federal floor is $7,000 — the same threshold used for FUTA, unchanged since 1983. States are free to set their own cap higher, and most do. For 2026, state wage bases run from $7,000 at the low end to more than $70,000 at the high end, and roughly half of all states tie their base to average wages or trust-fund health so it adjusts automatically each year.

The lowest 2026 wage bases — $7,000 — are in Arkansas, California, Florida, Louisiana, and Tennessee. Texas is close behind at $9,000. At the other end, Washington has the highest wage base in the country, and states such as Hawaii ($64,500) and Idaho ($58,300) are far above the federal floor. A high wage base means SUTA keeps applying to more of each employee's pay, so the same rate costs more in a high-base state than in a low-base one.

Our state guides give the figures for the states we have covered in depth: California's wage base is $7,000 and its new-employer rate is 3.4% — see our California payroll taxes guide — and Texas's wage base is $9,000 with a 2.7% new-employer rate — see our Texas payroll taxes guide.

Your rate: new-employer rate, then experience rating

Your SUTA rate is not a flat percentage. It is assigned to you, and it changes with your history:

  • New employers get a default rate. A new business has no claims history, so the state assigns a standard new-employer rate — often between 1% and 3%, and 2.7% in many states — until it has enough history for an experience-based rate, which typically takes two to four years. Some states vary the new-employer rate by industry. Examples for 2026: Texas 2.7%, Florida 2.7%, California 3.4%, New York 4.025%, Pennsylvania 3.822%, New Jersey 2.8%.
  • Then experience rating takes over. Your experience rating reflects how heavily former employees have drawn unemployment benefits charged to your account. If your workers rarely file claims, your rate drops over time. Significant layoffs push it higher. Across all states, rates generally range from fractions of a percent at the low end to around 6% to 8% at the high end, with a few states higher in specific cases.
  • Industry can matter. In some states the turnover volatility of your industry also plays a role in the assigned rate — construction and seasonal businesses, for example, often carry higher rates than stable office employers.

Your state sends you a rate notice each year, usually late in the year for the following year. Read it: the rate on that notice is the rate you pay, and it is the number to use when you budget payroll costs. If the notice looks wrong — for example, if it charges you for a former employee's claim you believe belongs to another employer — most states have an appeal process with a short deadline. Do not ignore it.

Worked example: the same employee, three different SUTA bills

Take one employee paid $45,000 a year. The SUTA cost depends entirely on the state:

Arizona (new employer)$8,0002.00%$160.00 (2.00% × $8,000)
Texas (new employer)$9,0002.70%$243.00 (2.70% × $9,000)
California (new employer)$7,0003.40%$238.00 (3.40% × $7,000)

The Arizona example is the telling one: the employee earns $45,000, but SUTA applies to only the first $8,000 of it. An experienced Arizona employer on the same employee could owe anywhere from $2.40 (0.03% × $8,000) to $668.80 (8.36% × $8,000) — the same worker, the same wages, a 279-fold difference driven entirely by experience rating. That is why two businesses in the same state, in the same industry, can pay wildly different SUTA: the rate is personal to the employer's claims history.

Now scale it to a team. A Texas employer with 10 employees, each paid $60,000, owes 10 × $243.00 = $2,430.00 in SUTA for the year at the new-employer rate — and the same employer owes 10 × $42.00 = $420.00 in FUTA. SUTA is usually the larger unemployment tax, and it is the one that varies. Run your own salary numbers on our payroll tax calculator.

How SUTA earns your FUTA credit — the $42 connection

This is the part of SUTA that most employers miss, and it is the most expensive to get wrong. FUTA, the federal unemployment tax, is 6.0% on the first $7,000 of each employee's wages — $420 per employee before credits. But employers who pay their state unemployment taxes on time and in full receive a credit of up to 5.4% against FUTA, which drops the effective federal rate to 0.6% — $42 per employee per year at most. Paying SUTA is what earns that credit.

Three things can shrink the credit:

  • You paid some SUTA late. Late state payments earn only 90% of the credit timely payment would have earned. Pay your state tax by the Form 940 due date to keep the full credit.
  • Some of your FUTA wages were excluded from state unemployment tax. In some states, wages paid to corporate officers, certain sick pay, or certain fringe benefits are excluded from state unemployment tax. On those wages you may owe FUTA at the full 6.0% rate.
  • Your state is a credit-reduction state. See the next section.

One reassurance: a low state rate does not cost you federal credit. If your state's experience rate is below 5.4%, you still get the full credit — the additional credit is the difference between what you actually paid and what you would have paid at 5.4%. The credit rewards paying the state, not paying a high state rate. For the full federal side, see our line-by-line Form 940 guide.

Credit-reduction states: when the federal bill goes up

A state becomes a credit-reduction state when it borrows from the federal government to pay unemployment benefits and fails to repay the loan within the required timeframe. Employers in that state lose part of the standard 5.4% FUTA credit, which raises their effective federal tax rate. The reduction starts at 0.3% for the first year a state qualifies and increases by an additional 0.3% for each subsequent year the loan remains unpaid.

Do not use last year's list. The Department of Labor redetermines credit-reduction states every November, and the IRS publishes the final Schedule A (Form 940) after that determination. For 2026, the Department's potential list names two jurisdictions: California, with a base reduction of 1.5% (which would make the total FUTA rate 2.1% there), and the U.S. Virgin Islands, at 4.8% (total FUTA rate 5.4%). California also faces a possible Benefit Cost Rate add-on of about 3.8%, which it has had waived every prior year — if the add-on were not waived, California employers' FUTA rate could reach 5.9%. The list was not final when this guide was written on November 15, 2026. Before you file, check the current Schedule A (Form 940) and the IRS FUTA credit reduction page (linked in Sources) and use the list printed on the form itself.

If you do pay wages in a credit-reduction state, you must complete Schedule A (Form 940) and the additional tax goes on Form 940, line 11. It counts as fourth-quarter liability for deposit purposes. Multi-state employers must file Schedule A even if none of their states is a credit-reduction state.

Who owes SUTA, and when to register

Under federal law, you generally owe unemployment tax if you paid at least $1,500 in wages during any calendar quarter or had at least one employee for some part of a day in 20 or more different weeks during the year. States follow similar thresholds, though some set their own triggers — check with your state workforce agency as soon as you hire your first employee, not at the end of the quarter.

Registering is a separate step from getting your federal EIN, and it is easy to miss. You apply for a state employer identification number (SEIN) through your state's department of labor or workforce agency — in California that is the Employment Development Department (EDD), in Texas the Texas Workforce Commission (TWC). Registration deadlines vary: Texas, for example, requires registration within 10 days of becoming liable. Once registered, you file quarterly wage reports and pay the tax each quarter, in the state where the employee performs the work — not the state where your business is headquartered. Remote workers count where they work.

Nonprofit organizations described in section 501(c)(3), state and local governments, and federally recognized Indian tribal governments are generally exempt from FUTA, and many states have parallel exemptions or a reimbursement option for SUTA — check your state's rules rather than assuming the federal exemption carries over automatically.

SUTA dumping: do not game the new-employer rate

Some employers have tried to escape a high experience rate by shifting their workforce to a newly created entity that qualifies for a clean, low new-employer rate. That is SUTA dumping, and states treat it as evasion, not planning. If it is found, the rate can be recalculated retroactively, with penalties and interest — and the federal government can also pursue it. The legitimate way to lower your SUTA rate is the slow one: fewer layoffs, fewer claims charged to your account, and time. If your rate seems wrong, appeal the rate notice or ask the agency to review the claims charged to you; do not restructure your payroll to chase a lower rate.

5 mistakes that cost employers money on SUTA

  1. Registering late — or not at all. Your EIN does not register you with the state. Register with your state workforce agency when you hire your first employee; some states give you as little as 10 days, and late registration can mean back taxes, penalties, and interest.
  2. Paying SUTA late and losing FUTA credit. Late state payments earn only 90% of the federal credit. The state penalty is bad enough; the shrunken federal credit is the hidden second cost. Pay the state first, then file Form 940.
  3. Paying SUTA in the wrong state. SUTA goes to the state where the employee works, not where the company is headquartered. Remote and multi-state workers are the usual trap — get the work location right on the quarterly wage report.
  4. Ignoring the annual rate notice. Your rate changes every year, and it is set by the state, not by your payroll software's default. If you budget last year's rate, you will be wrong — and if the notice charges you for a claim that is not yours, the appeal window is short.
  5. Using last year's credit-reduction list. The Department of Labor redetermines credit-reduction states every November. Filing Form 940 with a stale list — in either direction — produces the wrong line 11 and the wrong total tax.

Frequently asked questions

Q: What is SUTA tax?
A: SUTA is the State Unemployment Tax Act — the state unemployment insurance tax employers pay to fund benefits for workers who lose their jobs through no fault of their own. Each state runs its own program and sets its own rates and wage bases. It is separate from FUTA, the federal unemployment tax, and it is filed and paid quarterly to your state unemployment agency.

Q: Who pays SUTA — the employer or the employee?
A: The employer pays SUTA in almost every state; nothing is withheld from employees' wages. Only three states require employee contributions: Alaska, New Jersey, and Pennsylvania. In those states the employee share is small and separate from the employer rate.

Q: How is my SUTA rate set?
A: Two numbers set it: your state's taxable wage base and your experience rating. New employers get a default new-employer rate (often 1% to 3%, commonly 2.7%) until they build claims history, usually two to four years. After that, your rate rises or falls with how many former employees drew benefits charged to your account — fewer claims means a lower rate over time.

Q: What is the SUTA taxable wage base?
A: The maximum amount of each employee's annual wages subject to SUTA. For 2026, state wage bases run from $7,000 at the low end (California, Florida, Texas and others) to more than $70,000 in Washington, the highest. Once an employee's earnings pass the base, you stop owing SUTA on that worker for the rest of the year.

Q: How does SUTA affect my FUTA tax?
A: Paying your state unemployment tax on time and in full earns you a credit of up to 5.4% against the 6.0% federal FUTA rate, leaving a net FUTA rate of 0.6% — $42 per employee per year on the first $7,000 of wages. Paying SUTA late shrinks the credit; in a credit-reduction state the credit is reduced and you owe more FUTA.

Q: When and how do I pay SUTA?
A: SUTA is filed and paid quarterly to your state unemployment agency, on the state's quarterly wage report, in the state where the employee works — not where your business is headquartered. Register with the state workforce agency when you hire your first employee and get a state employer identification number.

Q: What is SUTA dumping?
A: Shifting workers to a newly created entity to get a clean, low new-employer rate and escape a high experience rate. States and the IRS treat it as evasion: rates can be recalculated retroactively, with penalties and interest. Do not restructure payroll to chase a lower SUTA rate.

Q: What happens if my state is a FUTA credit-reduction state?
A: Your 5.4% FUTA credit shrinks and you owe additional federal unemployment tax, figured on Schedule A (Form 940). The Department of Labor names the states each year after November 10. For 2026, California and the U.S. Virgin Islands are the expected credit-reduction jurisdictions — check the final 2026 Schedule A before you file.
