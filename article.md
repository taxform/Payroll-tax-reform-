## Who this is for

This is a one-page reference for U.S. employers, payroll managers, accountants, and HR teams who need the 2026 federal payroll tax numbers in one place — the rates, the wage caps, and the filing and deposit deadlines — with every figure traceable to the IRS or the Social Security Administration (SSA).

If you only need one number, it is in the tables below. If you need the full employer cost at a given salary, see “Worked employer-cost examples” and the companion study, [Employer Payroll Tax by Salary (2026)](https://payrolltaxreform.com/research/employer-payroll-tax-by-salary-2026.html), which gives the federal employer cost for 25 salary levels from $20,000 to $500,000.

> **A note on timing:** This reference was verified on November 11, 2026. The Q1–Q3 2026 Form 941 deadlines have already passed; the deadlines still ahead are the Q4 2026 Form 941, the 2026 Form 940, and the 2026 Forms W-2/W-3, all due February 1, 2027 (see the deadlines table).

## Key findings

- **A $50,000 employee costs the employer $3,867 in federal payroll taxes in 2026** — 7.73% on top of salary, before state taxes and benefits.
- **The employer’s effective federal rate falls as pay rises:** 7.86% on a $20,000 salary, 7.67% at the $184,500 Social Security wage base, and 3.75% on a $500,000 salary — because Social Security stops at the wage base while Medicare does not.
- **The Social Security wage base rose 4.8% in 2026**, from $176,100 to $184,500. The most an employer can owe in Social Security tax for one employee rose $520.80, to **$11,439.00**.
- **The rates themselves did not change in 2026.** Social Security is still 6.2% each side and Medicare is still 1.45% each side; what moves each year is the wage base.

## Table 1 — 2026 federal payroll tax rates and wage bases (employer and employee)

| Tax | Employer rate | Employee rate (withheld) | 2026 wage base / cap | Max employer tax per employee, 2026 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Social Security (OASDI) | 6.2% | 6.2% | $184,500 | $11,439.00 |
| Medicare (hospital insurance) | 1.45% | 1.45% | No cap — all covered wages | No maximum |
| Additional Medicare Tax | None — employer does not match it | 0.9% on wages over $200,000 (see below) | No cap | Not applicable |
| FUTA (federal unemployment) | 6.0% gross, **0.6% net** with the full 5.4% state credit | None — employer pays FUTA from its own funds | First $7,000 of wages per employee | **$42.00** with the full credit ($420.00 gross before credit) |

Sources: IRS Publication 15 (2026), “What’s New — Social security and Medicare taxes for 2026”; IRS Topic No. 751; SSA, Contribution and Benefit Base. Full source list at the end of this page.

### What each line means

- **Social Security, 6.2% each side (12.4% total).** Both the employer and the employee pay 6.2% on wages up to $184,500. Once an employee’s year-to-date wages pass $184,500, Social Security withholding stops for the rest of the calendar year — for both sides — and restarts on January 1.
- **Medicare, 1.45% each side (2.9% total).** There is no wage base limit for Medicare tax; all covered wages are subject to it.
- **Additional Medicare Tax, 0.9%, employee only.** The employer must withhold an extra 0.9% from wages paid to an employee in excess of **$200,000 in a calendar year**, starting in the pay period in which wages first exceed $200,000 and continuing until year-end. There is no employer share. The employer’s $200,000 withholding threshold applies without regard to filing status.
- **FUTA, 6.0% gross / 0.6% net.** FUTA is 6.0% on the first $7,000 of each employee’s annual wages. Employers can generally take a credit of up to 5.4% for amounts paid into state unemployment funds, leaving a net rate of 0.6% — $42.00 per employee per year. Employers in a FUTA credit-reduction state receive a smaller credit and pay more; the U.S. Department of Labor announces credit-reduction states each year, and the reduction is figured on Schedule A (Form 940). Do not withhold FUTA from employee wages.

## Table 2 — Additional Medicare Tax: employer withholding vs. the employee’s own liability

These two thresholds are different, and confusing them is a common error.

| | Employer withholding | Employee’s own tax liability |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rate | 0.9% | 0.9% |
| Threshold | **$200,000** of Medicare wages paid in the calendar year, regardless of filing status | Depends on filing status (see below) |
| Who pays it | The employee, through withholding; the employer does not match it | The employee, figured on Form 8959; any shortfall or excess vs. withholding is settled on the individual return |

**Employee liability thresholds (not indexed for inflation):**

| Filing status | Threshold |
| --- | --- |
| Married filing jointly | $250,000 |
| Married filing separately | $125,000 |
| Single | $200,000 |
| Head of household | $200,000 |
| Qualifying surviving spouse | $200,000 |

Sources: IRS Topic No. 751 (employer withholding at $200,000); IRS Topic No. 560 and Instructions for Form 8959 (filing-status thresholds).

Example: a married employee filing jointly who earns $220,000 has $180.00 withheld (0.9% × the $20,000 above $200,000). The employee’s own liability threshold is $250,000, so no Additional Medicare Tax is ultimately owed on $220,000 alone — the $180 withheld is reconciled on Form 8959. Conversely, a married employee filing separately who earns $150,000 has nothing withheld (under the $200,000 employer threshold) but owes 0.9% on the $25,000 above the $125,000 threshold. Employers should not try to adjust withholding for an employee’s filing status; the $200,000 rule is the employer’s rule.

### Deposit schedules — separate from filing

Filing a return and depositing the tax are separate steps. The deposit schedule is set by the lookback period — for Form 941, the 12 months (four quarters) from July 1 of the second preceding year through June 30 of the prior year.

| Deposit schedule | Who qualifies | When deposits are due |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Monthly | $50,000 or less in employment taxes reported in the lookback period (new employers start monthly) | By the 15th day of the following month |
| Semiweekly | More than $50,000 in the lookback period | Paydays Wed–Fri: deposit by the following Wednesday. Paydays Sat–Tue: deposit by the following Friday |
| $100,000 next-day rule | Any depositor | If you accumulate $100,000 or more in taxes on any day during a deposit period, deposit by the next business day |
| FUTA deposits | All FUTA employers | If FUTA liability is $500 or less in a quarter, carry it to the next quarter. Once it reaches $500 or more, deposit by the last day of the first month after the quarter ends. If it is $500 or less for Q4, pay it with Form 940 by January 31 (February 1, 2027, for 2026) |

All federal tax deposits must be made electronically, for example through EFTPS. Source: IRS Topic No. 757; IRS, Employment Tax Due Dates.

## Worked employer-cost examples

One employee, paid the full annual salary in calendar year 2026. **Employer share only** — Social Security 6.2% on wages up to $184,500, Medicare 1.45% on all wages, FUTA at the net 0.6% on the first $7,000 ($42.00). State unemployment insurance, state/local payroll taxes, workers’ compensation, and benefits are **not** included. The employee pays a matching 6.2% + 1.45% through withholding, plus 0.9% Additional Medicare Tax on wages over $200,000 (employee only; the employer does not match it).

| Annual salary | Social Security (employer) | Medicare (employer) | FUTA (net) | Employer federal total | Effective rate | Salary + employer federal taxes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $50,000 | $3,100.00 | $725.00 | $42.00 | **$3,867.00** | 7.73% | $53,867.00 |
| $60,000 | $3,720.00 | $870.00 | $42.00 | **$4,632.00** | 7.72% | $64,632.00 |
| $100,000 | $6,200.00 | $1,450.00 | $42.00 | **$7,692.00** | 7.69% | $107,692.00 |
| $184,500 | $11,439.00 | $2,675.25 | $42.00 | **$14,156.25** | 7.67% | $198,656.25 |
| $200,000 | $11,439.00 | $2,900.00 | $42.00 | **$14,381.00** | 7.19% | $214,381.00 |
| $250,000 | $11,439.00 | $3,625.00 | $42.00 | **$15,106.00** | 6.04% | $265,106.00 |
| $500,000 | $11,439.00 | $7,250.00 | $42.00 | **$18,731.00** | 3.75% | $518,731.00 |

### Reading the examples

- **$60,000 salary:** the employer pays $4,632.00 in federal payroll taxes. That is the figure behind the site’s [True Cost of an Employee](https://payrolltaxreform.com/blog/true-cost-of-an-employee.html) article, which adds state unemployment to reach $64,772–$67,032 in mandatory payroll taxes alone. A common planning rule is to budget 1.1× to 1.25× salary for the fully loaded cost of a hire, including benefits.
- **$184,500 — the wage-base row:** this is the last salary at which the full 6.2% Social Security match applies. Above it, only Medicare continues.
- **$250,000 salary:** the employee also has 0.9% Additional Medicare Tax withheld on the $50,000 above $200,000 — **$450.00**, paid by the employee, not matched by the employer. At $500,000 the employee’s Additional Medicare withholding is $2,700.00 (0.9% × $300,000).
- **Why the effective rate falls:** at $20,000 the employer rate is 7.86%; at $500,000 it is 3.75%. The $42 FUTA charge and the capped Social Security tax become a smaller share of a larger salary. For the full 25-salary table, see [Employer Payroll Tax by Salary (2026)](https://payrolltaxreform.com/research/employer-payroll-tax-by-salary-2026.html).

## Methodology

- **Scope:** federal payroll taxes only — Social Security, Medicare, Additional Medicare Tax, and FUTA — for wages paid in calendar year 2026. State unemployment insurance, state income-tax withholding, state/local payroll taxes, workers’ compensation, and benefits are excluded because they vary by state and employer.
- **Calculation:** for each salary, Social Security = 6.2% × min(salary, $184,500); Medicare = 1.45% × salary; FUTA (net) = 0.6% × min(salary, $7,000), assuming the full 5.4% state credit. Figures are rounded to the cent. The calculation is reproducible from the published rates in Table 1.
- **Deadlines:** standard due dates are the last day of the month after the quarter or year ends, moved to the next business day when they fall on a weekend or legal holiday (IRS Publication 509; IRS, Employment Tax Due Dates). Extended filing dates are the 10th day of the second month after the return period ends, moved likewise where that day is a weekend.
- **Verification:** every rate, wage base, threshold, and deadline in this reference was checked against an IRS or SSA primary source on November 11, 2026. The full source list is below. No figure is taken from a secondary roundup, a payroll vendor, or an AI summary.
- **What this page does not do:** it does not cover farm workers (Form 943), household employers (the $3,000 household-wage threshold in Publication 15 is a separate rule), railroad retirement taxes, or state requirements. It does not give tax advice for a specific employer.

## Limitations

- **FUTA credit-reduction states pay more.** The $42.00 FUTA figure assumes the full 5.4% credit. In a credit-reduction state the credit is smaller (reduced by 0.3% per year the state has an outstanding federal loan, and more in later years), so the net FUTA rate rises — for example, 0.9% with a 0.3% reduction. This reference does not list 2026 credit-reduction states; check the Department of Labor’s FUTA Credit Reductions page and Schedule A (Form 940) before filing.
- **Figures change every year.** The Social Security wage base is adjusted annually; the 2027 base is already published at $192,300. Re-verify rates and deadlines in IRS Publication 15 and Publication 509 for the following year before using this page for 2027 payroll.
- **Deposit rules have exceptions.** The monthly/semiweekly summary above is the standard rule; the $100,000 next-day rule, the accuracy-of-deposits rule, and special rules for new employers can change an individual employer’s due dates. See Publication 15, Section 11.
- **This is not tax advice.** Confirm figures with the IRS, SSA, your state agencies, and your payroll professional before filing or relying on them for a specific employee.
